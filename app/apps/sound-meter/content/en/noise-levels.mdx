"The room next door measured 50 dB." "The roadside was 70 dB." Numbers like these are hard to picture, and because decibels don't add up the way everyday numbers do, it is easy to misjudge how big a difference really is. This guide explains what a decibel is, how to read differences between values, how loud common sounds are, what residential noise standards look like, and how much exposure your ears can take. It ends with how to [measure with the Sound Level Meter](/) and make sense of what you see.

## What is a decibel (dB)?

### A ratio to a reference, on a logarithmic scale

Sound is a tiny, rapid fluctuation in air pressure. Sound pressure level compares the size of that fluctuation with a very small reference pressure (20 micropascals, roughly the quietest sound a person can hear) and expresses the ratio on a logarithmic scale. The unit is the decibel. A sound exactly as strong as the reference is 0 dB, so 0 dB does not mean "no sound."

Human hearing covers an enormous range: the loudest sounds we can tolerate have about a million times the pressure of the faintest ones we can detect. A logarithmic scale squeezes that range into something manageable, roughly 0 to 120 dB.

### Small numeric changes, big changes in energy

Because the scale is logarithmic, a difference in decibels is a multiplication, not an addition.

| Change | Sound energy | Sound pressure | How it tends to feel |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| +3 dB | about 2× | about 1.4× | Just noticeably louder |
| +6 dB | about 4× | about 2× | Clearly louder |
| +10 dB | 10× | about 3.2× | Roughly twice as loud |
| +20 dB | 100× | 10× | Roughly four times as loud |

Two rules of thumb are worth remembering:

- **+3 dB means double the energy.** Two equally loud sources together are about 3 dB louder than one. Two 60 dB vacuum cleaners make about 63 dB, not 120 dB.
- **+10 dB sounds roughly twice as loud.** The energy goes up tenfold, but most people perceive it as about twice as loud. Going from 50 dB to 60 dB is a bigger change than the numbers suggest.

Conversely, if one sound is 10 dB or more quieter than another, adding it barely changes the total (by less than 0.5 dB). That is why switching off the air conditioner next to a busy road hardly changes the reading.

### The "A" in dB(A)

Noise levels are often written as "dB(A)" or "dBA." Our ears are less sensitive to low and very high frequencies, and A-weighting discounts those frequencies so that the number follows perceived loudness more closely. Noise assessments and environmental standards are normally expressed in A-weighted values, and so are the figures in this guide. The differences between A, C and Z weighting are covered in [How to Measure Noise Accurately with Your Phone](/guide/how-to-measure/).

## How loud are everyday sounds?

The table below lists commonly quoted levels. Real values easily shift by 5–10 dB depending on the source, the model, the distance and how the room reflects sound, so treat these as rough guides only.

| Approximate level | Examples | Impression |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Around 20 dB | Rustling leaves, a ticking clock nearby | Barely audible |
| Around 30 dB | Whispering, a suburb late at night | Very quiet |
| Around 40 dB | Inside a library, a quiet residential street by day | Quiet |
| Around 50 dB | A quiet office, near an air-conditioner outdoor unit | Unobtrusive |
| Around 60 dB | Normal conversation, inside a quiet car on the move | Moderate |
| 60–70 dB | Vacuum cleaner, TV close up | Lively |
| Around 70 dB | Busy street, noisy office | Loud |
| Around 80 dB | Inside a train or subway car, a piano played nearby | Very loud |
| Around 90 dB | Shouting up close, a noisy factory floor, a karaoke room | Extremely loud |
| Around 100 dB | Under a railway bridge as a train passes | Uncomfortable |
| Around 110 dB | A car horn close by | Close to painful |
| Around 120 dB | Near an aircraft engine | Painful |

## Residential noise standards

### Japan's environmental quality standards as an example

Standards differ from country to country. As one example, Japan sets Environmental Quality Standards for Noise under its Basic Environment Act. They are expressed as the equivalent continuous sound level (LAeq, the energy average over a period) for each type of area and time of day. Daytime is 6:00 to 22:00 and night-time is 22:00 to 6:00.

| Area type | Typical area | Day | Night |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AA | Areas needing special quiet, such as clusters of hospitals and care facilities | 50 dB or less | 40 dB or less |
| A and B | Mainly residential areas | 55 dB or less | 45 dB or less |
| C | Mixed residential, commercial and industrial areas | 60 dB or less | 50 dB or less |

Areas facing major roads have separate, higher values. Which category an area falls into is decided by the local government, so check with your local authority for the exact figures where you live.

The World Health Organization's Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region (2018) take a different approach. For road traffic, they recommend keeping the long-term day-evening-night level below 53 dB and the night-time level below 45 dB. These figures use a different averaging method, so they cannot be compared one-to-one with a short measurement, but they give a sense of scale.

### Standards are targets, not limits on individual sounds

Environmental quality standards like Japan's are administrative goals for the environment of an area as a whole. They are not ceilings that apply directly to a neighbour's everyday noise or to a particular shop. Noise from factories, construction and vehicles is usually covered by separate noise control laws and local ordinances.

Even so, these values are a useful reference for what a quiet residential area sounds like. If your bedroom averages well above 45 dB at night, it is on the noisy side for a residential setting.

### Guidelines for sleep

The WHO's 1999 Guidelines for Community Noise suggest that, for good sleep, the average inside a bedroom should be around 30 dB(A), with individual noise events staying below about 45 dB(A). If night-time noise bothers you, look not only at the average but also at short loud events, such as passing cars or slamming doors (the maximum values). They often explain why you keep waking up.

## Loud sound over time strains your hearing

### 85 dB for 8 hours as a common reference

Long exposure to loud sound can damage the sensory cells in the inner ear and lead to noise-induced hearing loss. In most cases, hearing lost this way does not come back.

Occupational noise programs widely use **an 8-hour average of 85 dB(A)** as a key threshold. Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare guidelines on preventing noise-induced hearing loss, for example, treat 85 dB(A) as the point at which workplace noise calls for action.

Many guidelines also apply an "exchange rate" of 3 dB: every 3 dB increase doubles the energy, so the time it takes to reach the same dose is halved. Under that rule, the reference times look like this:

| Average level | Approximate daily limit |
| --- | --- |
| 85 dB(A) | 8 hours |
| 88 dB(A) | 4 hours |
| 91 dB(A) | 2 hours |
| 94 dB(A) | 1 hour |
| 100 dB(A) | 15 minutes |

These figures apply to healthy adults and are not a guarantee of safety below the line. Concerts, clubs, karaoke rooms and construction sites can easily exceed 90–100 dB.

### Headphones and earbuds count too

For personal listening, the WHO recommends that adults keep to about 80 dB for no more than 40 hours a week. Turning up the volume to drown out a noisy train easily goes beyond that. Using noise cancelling so you can listen at a lower volume, and giving your ears regular breaks, both help.

Ringing in the ears or muffled hearing after loud sound is a sign that your ears are fatigued. If it doesn't go away, see an ear, nose and throat doctor.

## Measure the sound around you

The easiest way to get a feel for these numbers is to measure for yourself. [Measure with the Sound Level Meter](/) to turn the sound around you into an estimated dB(A) value using your phone's or computer's microphone. Audio is analysed on your device only. Nothing is recorded or sent (see the [privacy policy](/privacy/)).

### Steps

1. Choose a duration of 5, 10, 30 or 60 seconds. For steady sound, 10 seconds is enough. For sound that changes, like passing traffic, use 30–60 seconds.
2. Tap "Start measuring." The first time, your browser asks for permission to use the microphone.
3. Keep the device still and don't cover the microphone.
4. When it finishes, you'll see the average (Leq), maximum, minimum, L10, L50, L90, the dominant frequency and a breakdown by frequency band.

### What to look at

- **Average (Leq)**: the representative level over the period. This is the value to compare with environmental standards.
- **Maximum (Lmax)**: brief loud events such as a passing car or a door closing. Useful when sleep is being disturbed.
- **L90**: the level exceeded 90% of the time. It filters out voices and other short events and indicates the background noise of the place.
- **Frequency bands**: a large share of low frequencies points to sources such as outdoor AC units, engines or footsteps.

Measuring the same room by day and at night, or with the window open and closed, quickly shows where the noise comes from and whether a fix is working. For more on what each value means and when to use it, see [How to Measure Noise Accurately with Your Phone](/guide/how-to-measure/).

### Phone readings are estimates

Phone and computer microphones are not measuring instruments. The Sound Level Meter adds a device-specific offset to the level the microphone picks up. On iPhone and iPad, the default offset was set by measuring side by side with a calibrated sound meter app. On other devices it is a provisional value. Readings can be off by a few decibels, and sometimes by 10 dB or more.

If you have a commercial sound level meter or a calibrated app, measure together in the same place and enter its value under "Match a reference (calibrate)" below your results to adjust the offset. Where the number really matters, though, such as a formal complaint or a dispute with a neighbour, you need readings from a certified sound level meter. Some local authorities lend meters to residents, so it's worth asking.

## Summary

- Decibels are logarithmic. +3 dB doubles the energy and +10 dB sounds roughly twice as loud.
- A quiet residential street by day is around 40 dB, normal conversation around 60 dB, and the inside of a train around 80 dB.
- In Japan, the standard for mainly residential areas is about 55 dB by day and 45 dB at night, though it varies by area and proximity to roads.
- 85 dB(A) for 8 hours a day is a common reference, and every 3 dB more halves the safe time.
